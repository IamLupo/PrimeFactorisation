====================================================================================================
INTERPRETATION
====================================================================================================

The previous experiments established that the moduli

    3,5,7,11,13,17,19,23

are all divisors of M.

Consequently, if two prime pairs satisfy

    a*b == p*q (mod M),

then they necessarily have exactly the same residue under every
combination of those eight moduli.

This experiment changes only one thing:

    NEW primes which do NOT divide M.

For example:

    29,31,37,41,...

Now two members of the same C-class can have different fingerprints.

The direct product fingerprint is

    (a*b mod r1,
     a*b mod r2,
     ...)

and is exactly the multi-modulus version of the original

    n mod r

idea.

A class is "resolved" when all of its members have distinct
fingerprints.

Because the observed C-classes contain at most a few members, this
experiment can directly determine how many foreign moduli are needed
to identify every member within a class.

There are three possible outcomes.

1. ONE foreign modulus frequently resolves the class.

   Then even a single modulus outside M carries substantial
   discriminatory information.

2. TWO OR THREE foreign moduli are usually enough.

   Then the fingerprint idea works as a compact identifier of the
   finite C-class.

3. Some classes remain unresolved even with all foreign moduli.

   Then those classes possess genuine product-residue collisions
   extending across the larger modulus set.

The important distinction is:

    divisors of M
        test the same congruence information

whereas:

    primes not dividing M
        test independent residue information.

For the shifted relation

    a*b = p*q + 2*M*t,

a foreign modulus r gives

    a*b - p*q = 2*M*t (mod r).

Because r does not divide M, the right-hand side generally does NOT
vanish modulo r.

Therefore foreign moduli can actually see the shift.

That is exactly the direction required to return to the original
fingerprint hypothesis.

This experiment still does not establish a factoring algorithm.

It establishes something more basic first:

    whether a small collection of independent modular fingerprints
    can uniquely identify the relevant prime-pair state inside the
    exact M-congruence class.
